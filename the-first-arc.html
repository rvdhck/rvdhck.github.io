Four things to know before anything else

Your actual numbers — measured 3 September

You're 13 — 14 later this month — 160 cm and 43 kg. That's a BMI of about 16.8, which sits around the 15th percentile for your age. In plain terms: you're thin, at the low end of normal, but you are not clinically underweight.

An earlier version of this page said you were below the 3rd percentile and told you to book a GP appointment before starting. That was calculated from an estimated 40 kg, and at your real weight it was too strong. There's no medical reason to see anyone before you begin.

What does still stand: if you follow the food plan properly for 8–10 weeks and the scale genuinely hasn't moved, that's worth a GP visit. Coeliac disease and an overactive thyroid are ordinary, easily-tested reasons a person can't gain weight, and it beats throwing more food at a problem that isn't about food. Worth showing this page to a parent either way — a second set of eyes on a plan you're following for six months is just sensible.

2. The training is the easy part. Every source in the research folder agrees on this and they're right. Three sessions a week is under three hours. 3. Being light is an advantage right now. Every exercise in here lifts your own bodyweight, and at 43 kg that's easier than it'll be at 50. Make the most of it — and read the bit in section 13 about what happens to your rep counts around month four, because it's going to look like bad news and it won't be.

4. There are no supplements in this plan. No protein powder, no creatine, no weight gainer. Four of the seven bulking videos recommend them. You're 13, and everything you actually need is available as food. If that changes later it's a conversation with a parent and a GP, not something to decide off a YouTube video.

And a trained 50 kg looks nothing like an untrained 50 kg. If most of those 7 kg arrive as muscle on a frame that currently carries almost none, the difference will read as much bigger than the number suggests.

"Muscle, not fat" — right idea, needs adjusting

Realistically, half to two-thirds of what you gain will be lean tissue and the rest will be fat. That's a good result, not a failure. Two things are working in your favour: research shows a beginner in a modest surplus can gain almost entirely lean tissue, and you're 13 and going through puberty, which is the most anabolic window you will ever get. Genuinely — this is the best time in your life to do this.

But 100% muscle isn't a thing at any age. And starting from BMI 16.8, gaining a bit of fat along the way is healthy — you're carrying almost no reserve right now.

One more thing: you'll probably grow taller over these six months. If you go from 160 to 163 cm, the weight target should rise with it — 50 kg at 163 cm is a BMI of 18.8 rather than 19.5, so it'll look leaner. Measure your height every 8 weeks alongside the weigh-in, and if you've grown, add about 1 kg to the target for every 1.5 cm.

Why not just eat more and go faster? Because the faster you gain, the worse the muscle-to-fat ratio gets. Muscle growth has a ceiling that food can't push past — once you're past it, the extra calories have nowhere to go except fat. Every credible source in the folder says the same thing: a 200–500 calorie surplus is the whole game. Going bigger is how people end up skinny-fat instead of lean.

The one honest limitation of this plan

Without a pull-up bar there's no vertical pulling in here. Table rows build back thickness well, but lat width — the V-taper that makes someone look big rather than just lean — comes mostly from pull-ups. This plan works around it by pushing shoulder volume, since wide delts also create width, and it will work. But if looking broad is specifically the goal, a doorway bar is the single highest-value thing you could own. Saying it once; the rest of the plan assumes you won't have one.

04The session

One workout, three times a week

Monday, Wednesday, Friday, around 4:15pm. One session, repeated — not two alternating ones. Keeping the exercises fixed means last Monday's number is the number to beat, and that is the entire mechanism this plan runs on. Leave at least one rest day between sessions.

How to read "3 × 8–12" in a pair

Block A is 6 sets in total, not 18. The "3 ×" counts how many times you go through the pair. One round = one set of pike push-ups plus one set of rows. Three rounds = 3 sets of each.

"No rest between the two" means no rest between the pike push-up and the row. It does not mean no rest between rounds. Here's the actual clock for block A:

So between pike set 1 and pike set 2 you actually get the row (~35 s) plus the 2-minute rest ≈ 2 minutes 35 seconds of shoulder recovery. That's plenty. Pairing buys you time; it doesn't cost you recovery.

Total time: 6 warm-up + 9 (A) + 9 (B) + 9 (C) + 10 (D) + 7 (E) + 4 cool-down = about 54 minutes.

There's no time limit on this session

An earlier version of this plan squeezed everything into 45 minutes and had to short-change your chest to do it. That's gone. The session runs about 55 minutes now and every muscle group gets proper volume. If a session overruns because you needed an extra minute of rest, fine — rest isn't wasted time, it's what makes the next set count. Never cut rest short to finish quicker. Cut a set instead.

How hard to push

First two sets of everything: stop 1–2 reps before failure. You should finish knowing you had a couple left in you. Last set only: go until your form breaks, and stop there. Not past it.

This is deliberately different from the source material. The calisthenics channel pushes "rep promises" — picking a number above your max and forcing yourself past failure on every set. That's a reasonable tool for an adult with seven years of training behind them. For you, in your first structured programme, it just builds fatigue you can't recover from — and recovery is where a growing body actually builds muscle. Soreness isn't the scoreboard. The log is.

Volume — every group covered

This session is 30 sets. Across the week that lands at 18 sets each for chest, back, shoulders and legs, plus 9 for calves and 9 direct for core — on top of the heavy indirect core work in every wall walk, push and row. Your biceps get 9 direct sets from the underhand rows; triceps get 18 across the three pressing movements.

Everything sits inside the 10–20 sets per muscle per week band that the source material converges on.

Don't start at 30 sets

Thirty sets is the steady state, not week one. Weeks 1–2 you run 2 sets per exercise, not 3 — that's 20 sets — while you work out the right difficulty for each movement. The third set goes on in week 3. Jumping straight to full volume in your first structured programme buys you a fortnight of being too sore to train properly, and nothing else.

05Exercises

How to do all ten, and what usually goes wrong

Furniture safety — check this once, properly
  • The table. Before your first row, hang your full weight off the edge with both hands and see what happens. It must not creak, tip, slide or lift. Push the far side against a wall so it can't travel. No glass tops. No folding legs. No trestle tables. Grip near a leg, not in the middle of a long span — the middle is where tops flex and snap.
  • Chairs. No wheels, ever. No swivel. Four legs on the floor, back against a wall for step-ups.
  • Sofa. Firm front edge for hip hinges. If it slides, brace it against a wall.
  • Parallettes. On carpet or a mat, never smooth tile or laminate — they'll skate out from under you. Check they aren't rocking before every set.
  • No usable table? Lay a broom handle across two sturdy chairs and grip it close to the chairs, not in the middle. Test it with your weight before you trust it.

A1 · Pike push-up parallettes

Builds: shoulders, upper chest, triceps. Do it: hands on the parallettes, feet walked in towards them so your hips are high and your body makes an upside-down V. Lower the top of your head down and slightly behind your hands, not forward past them. Push back up until your arms lock.

Three mistakes: 1. Hips too low — that turns it into a decline push-up and stops training your shoulders. Check by looking backwards between your hands: you should be able to see your own feet. 2. Head travelling forward past your hands instead of down behind them. 3. Elbows flaring out sideways instead of tracking back at about 45°.

Too hard (under 6 reps)? Drop the parallettes, do pike push-ups with hands flat on the floor, build to 3 × 10, then come back. The extra depth parallettes add is a big jump.
Too easy (over 15)? Feet on a chair → feet on the sofa → eventually a wall handstand push-up.

A2 · Table row, overhand table

Builds: upper back, rear delts, lats, forearms. Do it: lie under the table, grip the edge overhand (palms facing away) a bit wider than your shoulders, legs straight, heels on the floor, body in one rigid line from heel to head. Pull your shoulder blades back and down first, then drive your elbows back until your chest touches the edge. Lower under control — 3 seconds down, fast up.

Three mistakes: 1. Hips sagging — it becomes an arm exercise. Squeeze your glutes and hold a plank the whole way. 2. Shrugging your shoulders up to your ears. Blades go back and down before your arms do anything, every single rep. 3. Half range — your chest has to actually reach the table edge.

Too hard? Bend your knees and put your feet flat. That shortens the lever a lot.
Too easy? Feet up on a chair → add a 5-second lowering.

B1 · Deficit push-up parallettes

Builds: chest, triceps, front delts. Do it: hands on the parallettes, body in a straight line. Because your hands are raised, your chest can travel below them — that extra range is the entire point, and it's why this works now that floor push-ups don't. Go down until your chest is level with or below the grips. Elbows at roughly 45° to your body, not flared to 90°.

Three mistakes: 1. Not using the extra depth — stopping where a floor push-up would stop wastes the equipment completely. 2. Hips sagging or piking up. Straight line, ribs down. 3. Elbows flared wide, which loads your shoulder joint instead of your chest.

Too hard? Put the parallettes on a low step, or go back to floor push-ups with a slow 3-second lowering.
Too easy? Feet on a chair → feet on the sofa → add a 3-second lowering → archer push-ups.

B2 · Table row, underhand table

Builds: lats, upper back and biceps — which is why there are no curls in this plan. An underhand row trains your biceps harder than curls do and costs no extra time. Do it: exactly like A2 but with palms facing towards you, hands about shoulder-width. Drive your elbows down and back, squeezing them towards each other, and pull your hands to your chest. 3 seconds down, fast up.

The mistake that matters: pulling with your arms only and forgetting your shoulder blades. Same cue as A2 — blades back and down first, then the elbows move. If you only feel this in your biceps, you're skipping that step.

Too hard? Knees bent, feet flat.
Too easy? Feet on a chair → 3-second lowering.

C1 · Decline push-up, feet on the sofa sofa + parallettes

Builds: upper chest above all, plus front delts and triceps. This is the slot that fixes the flat-chest-with-arms look — raising your feet shifts the load onto the upper part of the pec, which is the bit that fills out the top of a t-shirt. Do it: parallettes on the floor, feet up on the sofa seat, body in one straight line, hands under your shoulders. Lower until your chest is level with or below the grips, then push back up.

Three mistakes: 1. Arching your lower back to fake extra depth. The source video calls this out specifically — it's why your hands are on parallettes here, so the range comes from the deficit and not from your spine. Squeeze your glutes, ribs down. 2. Feet too high too soon. Sofa-seat height is plenty; the back of the sofa is a much harder exercise. 3. Elbows flared to 90°. Same 45° rule as every other push in this plan.

Too hard? Feet on a low chair instead of the sofa, or hands flat on the floor without the parallettes.
Too easy? Add a 3-second lowering → feet on the sofa back → archer decline push-ups.

C2 · Wall walk wall

Builds: shoulders, upper traps, serratus, core — the source video describes it accurately as a front raise, an overhead press, a plank and a shrug all in one movement. Do it: start in a push-up position with your feet against the wall. Walk your feet up the wall while walking your hands back towards it, staying braced. Go as high as you can control, hold 2–3 seconds, then walk back down the same way. One up-and-down = one rep.

Two mistakes: 1. Arching your lower back as your hips rise. Squeeze your glutes, keep your ribs pulled down. 2. Going higher than you can walk back down from. Always keep enough left to reverse it under control.

Too hard? Do it with your hands on a chair instead of the floor, hinging at the hips — much easier.
Too easy? Walk closer to the wall and add pause-holds at 45° and vertical.

D1 · Split squat floor

Builds: quads, glutes. Do it: one long stride forward, back heel lifted. Drop straight down until your back knee is just above the floor and your front thigh is roughly parallel. Drive up through your front heel. All reps on one leg, then swap. Touch a wall or chair back with one finger for balance if you need to — that isn't cheating.

Three mistakes: 1. Stride too short, which turns it into a knee-dominant lunge and hurts. Go longer than feels natural. 2. Pushing off your back foot. The back leg is a kickstand; the front leg does the work. 3. Torso collapsing forward. Chest up, weight through the front heel.

Too hard? Shorten the range — go down only as far as you can control.
Too easy? Back foot on a chair (Bulgarian split squat) → step-ups onto a chair → assisted pistol squat holding a door frame.

D2 · Hip hinge, heels under the sofa sofa

Builds: hamstrings, glutes, lower back — the muscles nothing else in this session covers. Do it: kneel on a folded towel or mat with your heels wedged under the sofa. Keeping your body in one straight line from knees to head, lean your whole torso backwards, then lower forward under control as far as you can hold, feeling the stretch in your hamstrings. Pull back up using your hamstrings.

Two mistakes: 1. Bending at the hips instead of staying rigid — your body has to move as one plank, hinging at the knees. 2. Going too far too soon and dropping. Only go as far as you can come back from.

Too hard? Reduce the range and use your hands on the floor to catch yourself and push back up.
Too easy? Arms crossed on chest → arms straight overhead → full Nordic negatives.

E1 · Single-leg calf raise parallette or stair

Builds: calves — the one muscle group nothing else in this session touches, and the one the source material specifically warns about neglecting. Do it: stand on one foot with the ball of your foot on a parallette or the edge of a stair, heel hanging off in free air. Hold a wall or bannister for balance. Let your heel sink below the level of the step for a full stretch, then press all the way up onto your toes and pause for a second at the top. That bottom stretch is the whole reason for using an edge instead of the floor.

Two mistakes: 1. Bouncing. Calves are tendon-heavy and springy, so it's easy to let the Achilles do the work instead of the muscle. Slow down, pause at both ends. 2. Half range at the bottom — if your heel never drops below the step, you might as well be standing on the floor.

Too hard? Both feet at once on the step.
Too easy? Add a 3-second pause at the top, then a 3-second lowering. Reps run higher here than anywhere else in the plan (12–15) because calves respond to it.

E2 · Hollow body hold floor

Builds: the whole front of your core, properly. This replaces planks, which at 90 seconds are doing nothing for you. Do it: lie on your back, press your lower back flat into the floor and keep it there. Lift your shoulder blades and your legs a few inches off the ground, arms reaching past your ears. Hold. Your lower back must not lift.

The one mistake that matters: your lower back arching off the floor. The moment there's daylight under it, the exercise has stopped working and it's just a back strain waiting to happen. If that happens, tuck your knees in until you can hold it flat.

Too hard? Knees bent and tucked, or arms by your sides instead of overhead.
Too easy? Hollow rocks → tuck L-sit on the parallettes → one leg extended.

06Getting harder

One rule, four phases

The only progression rule you need

Hit the top of the rep range on all sets, two sessions in a row → make that exercise harder. In this order: add reps → add a 3-second lowering → move to the next variation.

Over 15 reps means it's too easy — move up. Under 6 reps means it's too hard — step back a rung. Move one slot at a time, never two in the same session.

The shoulder clicking on pike push-ups — sorted, but keep the cue
Status: fixed

Full pike push-ups on parallettes are working. The problem was never strength or range — it was your shoulder clicking, and you traced it to form and fixed it. Nothing in the programme needs changing. This section stays because clicking is the kind of thing that comes back, and when it does it helps to know why.

Why an overhead press makes a shoulder click. When your arm goes overhead, your shoulder blade has to rotate upwards to make room for it. If it can't — because your elbows are flared out to 90°, or because your shoulders are shrugged up towards your ears instead of set back and down — the top of your arm bone travels through a tighter space than it should, and tendons snap over bone on the way past. That's the click.

The cue that fixed it, and keeps it fixed:

  • Elbows track back at roughly 45°, never flared to 90°.
  • Shoulder blades set back and down before the rep starts, then allowed to rotate up naturally as your arms extend — no shrugging to your ears.
  • Head travels down and slightly behind the line of your hands, not forward past them.
  • Parallettes must not rock. On a mat or carpet, checked before each set. A grip that shifts mid-rep pulls your shoulder off its line.

Watch for it coming back at the end of a set. Form breaks under fatigue, and this is exactly the fault that creeps back on the last two or three reps when you're pushing hard. If the click reappears, it's almost always telling you the set should have ended a rep earlier — not that something is wrong with your shoulder.

The line between fine and not fine

Clicking without pain is common and generally harmless — noisy joints are normal, especially in a body that's still growing, and it doesn't mean damage. Clicking with pain is different. If it ever comes with pain during the movement, pain afterwards, weakness, or a catching feeling where your arm won't move smoothly through the range, stop pressing overhead and get it looked at. That's a GP or physio conversation, not something to train through.

Where 2,800 comes from

  • Resting metabolism (Schofield equation for boys 10–18): 17.686 × 43 kg + 658.2 = ~1,420 kcal/day
  • × 1.7 for school, walking around and three training sessions a week = ~2,410 kcal maintenance
  • + ~390 kcal surplus = ~2,800

That surplus sits inside the 200–500 kcal range every credible source in the folder agrees on. It should get you about 0.3 kg a week.

This went up from 2,700 when your measured weight came in at 43 kg rather than the estimated 40. A bigger body costs more to run — roughly 30 kcal per kg at your activity level, so 3 kg bought about 90 extra calories a day. Expect this number to creep up again as you gain: recalculate it at week 13 and again at week 26.

Protein: 90 g, not 190 g

Four of the seven bulking videos tell you to eat 1 gram of protein per pound of bodyweight. For you that's ~95 g now and ~110 g at goal weight — but they're quoting a rule built for 85 kg adults, where it lands at 190 g. For a 43 kg body, 90 g is already 2.1 g per kg, comfortably above the roughly 1.6 g/kg that maximises muscle growth. Chasing more would only crowd out the calories you're already struggling to eat. Protein is also the most filling of the three macronutrients, which for you is a cost, not a benefit. The plan below delivers about 120 g on paper — deliberately over target, because nobody finishes everything.

Ramp up — don't jump to 2,800 on day one

You've spent your whole life eating a certain amount, and doubling it overnight is how people end up nauseous and put off food for a week. Add 150 calories a day for the first two weeks — that's one shake's worth of peanut butter and a banana — then another 150, and so on. Reaching 2,800 by week five is a win, not a delay. Your appetite genuinely does expand; it just needs a few weeks, not a few days.

You're not going to track calories in an app

Three of the seven videos recommend MyFitnessPal. This plan deliberately doesn't. Logging every calorie every day is a habit with real risks at your age, and it isn't necessary — the weekly weigh-in tells you everything the app would, with none of the downside. Follow the meal structure, weigh yourself once a week, adjust from the scale.

09The shakes

Two a day — about 1,200 calories you don't have to chew

This is the thing that makes the whole plan work. Liquid empties from your stomach much faster than solid food and makes you far less full for the same calories. Every single source in the folder arrived at this independently — it's the one piece of advice they all agree on. Two shakes a day carry roughly 45% of your calories.

Four rules for shakes
  1. Blend the oats and dates first, dry or with a splash of milk, before anything else goes in. Otherwise you get lumps, and lumps are why people quit shakes.
  2. Drink it after food, never before. If you drink a shake first you'll be too full for dinner and you'll have gained nothing.
  3. Sip it, don't down it. A 750-calorie shake in one go is grim. In a flask over an hour it's easy.
  4. Blend the breakfast one the night before. Your appetite is worst in the morning, and a 6:30 alarm plus a blender is a plan that fails in week two.

How to actually make one

Same method for all four. The order matters more than anything else here — get it wrong and you get lumps, and lumps are the number one reason people give up on shakes.

  1. Dry things alone, first. Oats, dates and any nuts go into the blender on their own with no liquid. Blitz 20–30 seconds until it looks like coarse powder. Do not skip this. Oats and dates blended alongside liquid never break down properly — they just spin around and you end up chewing your drink.
  2. Half the milk in. Blend 10 seconds. You want a smooth paste before anything soft goes near it.
  3. Everything else in. Banana, yoghurt, nut butter, berries, honey, the teaspoon of olive oil if you're using it. Blend 30–45 seconds.
  4. The rest of the milk, to adjust. Too thick, add milk. Too thin, add more oats or a frozen banana. Thickness is personal — find yours and stick to it.
  5. Straight into a flask so you can sip it over an hour instead of forcing it down in one go.
Making it the night before

Blend it completely as above, pour into a sealed bottle, fridge. It will be noticeably thicker in the morning — the oats keep absorbing milk overnight, which is normal and not a sign it's gone off. Add a splash of milk and shake it hard before drinking.

It keeps comfortably for 12 hours. Any longer and the banana starts to taste flat, so make it at night for the morning, not two days ahead.

Three things that make it much better
  • Freeze the bananas. Peel, break into chunks, freeze in a bag. A frozen banana turns a thin shake into something that tastes like a proper milkshake, and costs nothing.
  • Rinse the blender immediately. Oats and nut butter set like cement in about twenty minutes. Ten seconds of rinsing now saves five minutes of scrubbing later, and "the blender is disgusting" is a real reason people stop making these.
  • No blender? Use instant or fine porridge oats, which need no blending at all. Mash the banana properly with a fork, chop the dates very small, and whisk hard in a jug. Not as smooth, but it works and it's still 700 calories.

Why food comes out bland — the six actual reasons

Bland food is almost never a case of "not enough spice in the jar." It's one of these six, and usually more than one at once. Fix them and the same ingredients taste completely different.

1. The spices never hit hot oil. This is the big one. Ground spices are locked up until fat and heat release them — that's why the powder stirred into a finished dish tastes dusty and slightly bitter, while the same powder cooked in oil for 45 seconds tastes like a restaurant. Always add ground spices to hot oil, off the boil, and stir for 30–60 seconds before any liquid goes in. A splash of water at that moment stops them catching.

2. Not enough salt. The single most common cause, and the hardest to accept. Salt doesn't make food salty until you've gone well past the right amount — before that it just makes food taste more like itself. Salt in layers: a pinch on the onions, a pinch when the protein goes in, then taste and adjust at the end. If a dish tastes flat and you can't work out why, it's salt about 80% of the time.

3. The onions aren't browned enough. Almost everyone stops too early. Pale onions give you nothing; properly golden-brown onions give you the sweetness and depth the whole dish is built on. That takes 8–12 minutes on medium heat, not two. Put a timer on it the first few times — you'll be surprised how long it actually is.

4. You stopped cooking the base too soon. There's a visual cue for when the masala is done: the oil separates and pools at the edges, and the paste pulls away from the pan. Before that happens it tastes raw and sharp. After it happens it tastes cooked and round. Wait for it every time — it's usually another 3–5 minutes past when you'd have stopped.

5. Nothing acidic at the end. A squeeze of lemon over a finished dish is the difference between "fine" and "why does this taste better than mine." Acid lifts everything and makes the other flavours legible. Lemon juice, or a splash of vinegar.

6. Nothing fresh or aromatic at the finish. Long cooking kills delicate aromas. Garam masala is a finishing spice — stir it in off the heat, in the last 30 seconds, not at the start with everything else. Same with fresh coriander leaves and sliced green chilli. This is the step that makes food smell like something before you've even tasted it.

Using the air fryer

The trap — read this before you use it

An air fryer is sold on one promise: the same food with far less oil. For you that is precisely the wrong feature. Two tablespoons of olive oil is 240 calories, and quietly removing it from every meal costs you roughly 250 calories a day — which is about two-thirds of your entire surplus. You'd stop gaining weight and never work out why.

So: toss everything in the full 2 tbsp of olive oil before it goes in the basket, exactly as if you were frying it. The air fryer cooks properly oiled food perfectly well — it just doesn't need the oil, which isn't the same as you not needing it. Ignore every "healthy air fryer" instinct the manual encourages.

One consequence worth knowing: because fat drips away from meat into the drawer, keep the beef mince on the hob. Air frying it would drain off exactly the fat you paid for.

What it genuinely improves. The air fryer replaces your oven and your frying pan, not your saucepan. It's better than a pan at potatoes, chicken pieces, patties and fish — hotter, more even, crispier edges, and it needs no attention while it runs, which means you can build the sauce on the hob at the same time. That's the real win: two things cooking at once instead of one.

What it can't do. The masala base needs a pan — you're browning onions in oil and building a sauce, and moving air is the wrong tool for that. Same for rice, and for anything with much liquid in it. So most dinners below become a two-appliance job: air fryer for the solid component, hob for the sauce, combined at the end.

12The appetite problem

Your actual bottleneck, and what to do about it

You fill up fast. That's the constraint this entire plan is designed around, and it's harder in your case than in any of the source videos — because their universal answer is pizza, and that answer isn't available here.

What works

  • Drink calories, don't eat them. Two shakes a day, roughly 1,200 calories. This is the big one.
  • Liquid after solid food, never before. Straight out of the Austin Dunham transcript and it's the best tactic in the folder. Eat the meal, then drink.
  • Add fat to finished food. Oil through rice, cheese on eggs, butter on chapati. Calories with no volume.
  • Six smaller occasions, not three big ones. Three large meals means three chances to hit the wall and stop.
  • No water in the 30 minutes before a meal. 1.5–2 litres a day, but spread out and away from mealtimes. Water is filling and has no calories — the worst possible combination for you.
  • Dried fruit over fresh. Same fruit, water removed, a third of the volume for the same calories.
  • Fixed meal times with phone reminders. Don't wait until you feel hungry. Your hunger signal is the thing that's broken — you can't use it as the trigger.
  • Never skip an occasion because you're not hungry. A missed one is 400–700 calories you cannot make up later in the day.
Two bits of source advice this plan rejects

"Set a 5-minute timer and eat as fast as possible before your brain notices." It technically works — the full signal lags about 20 minutes behind. But training yourself to race past your own fullness cues is a genuinely bad habit to install, and it's the kind of thing that doesn't switch off cleanly later. Eat at a normal pace, more often. If you want one behavioural nudge, use "eat without your phone" — that improves your attention to food instead of overriding it.

"Drink lots of water to stretch your stomach so you can eat more." This is backwards. Water fills your stomach and kills your appetite; it doesn't expand your capacity. The same folder elsewhere correctly says not to drink before meals. Follow that version.

And one more from the folder

One video suggests cutting back on household chores to conserve calories. Ignore it. The calories saved are trivial, the message is bad, and a 13-year-old who stops helping around the house because of a training plan isn't going to be on that training plan for long.

The month-four trap — expect it, don't panic

Around month four your rep counts are going to look like they've stopped moving. You'll think you've plateaued. You haven't.

Every exercise in this plan lifts your own bodyweight. By month four you should be around 48 kg, which means every single rep is lifting 12% more than it was in week one — and by 50 kg it's 16% more. Holding 10 reps at 48 kg is meaningfully stronger than 10 reps at 43 kg; it just doesn't look like it in the log. Weight up, reps flat is a good outcome. Straight answer first: here's every way to get the number, what it costs, and how wrong it is.

Every online body fat calculator is built for adults

This matters more than the error bars. The standard formulas — Jackson-Pollock, Durnin-Womersley, the Navy one — were developed and validated on adults. Body composition shifts fast and unevenly during puberty, so those equations systematically misread teenagers.

The ones actually validated for under-18s are the Slaughter equations, which use triceps and calf skinfolds. If you ever do want a real number, that's the method to ask for by name. Almost no free online calculator uses it.

About that 8%

Two things worth knowing about the number you're picturing.

It's mostly not real. 8% is competition-day bodybuilder condition — held for a few days, not a way of living. Most people online claiming 8% are somewhere around 12–15% and either guessing or using a scale that flatters them. For reference: visible abs on a man usually start showing somewhere around 12–15%, not 8%.

And for you specifically, it's the wrong target. A healthy range for a teenage boy is roughly 10–20%. Going below about 8% while you're still growing isn't an achievement, it's a problem — it interferes with hormone production, growth, and immune function. Your body needs some fat to do the very thing you're training for.

The thing nobody tells you about your situation

You said it yourself in the original brief: you have no belly fat, and that's why the abs won't show. That's the whole answer. Your abs aren't hidden under fat — there isn't any. They aren't visible because there isn't enough muscle there yet to see.

So for you, abs are a muscle-building problem, not a fat-loss problem. That's genuinely good news, because it means the fix is the hollow holds and the progressive overload in this plan, not a diet. Trying to get leaner would take you in exactly the wrong direction — and you're on a bulk, so your body fat is going to tick up slightly over the next six months. That's the plan working, not the plan failing.

What to track instead. The four things in this section already tell you everything a body fat percentage would, with no error bars and no cost: the weekly weight, the strength log, the photo every 8 weeks, and — if you want one fat-specific number — your waist measured at the belly button, same time of day, once a month. If your weight is climbing and your waist isn't, the gain is going where you want it. That single comparison is more useful than any percentage you could buy.

Revisit the question at 50 kg. Once you've got the muscle on, knowing your body fat actually becomes useful, because then there's something to reveal.
